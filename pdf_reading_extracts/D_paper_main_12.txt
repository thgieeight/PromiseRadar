Abstract
This paper studies a lightweight selective-routing framework for CPU-only inference on
resource-constrained devices. The system keeps a small always-on guard active for routine
inputs and selectively activates stronger experts only when a routing signal indicates difficulty.
Rather than introducing a new model family, the work studies a model-agnostic routing policy
built around a guard, an expert stage, and a cost-aware trigger.

The main empirical finding is regime-dependent. In balanced settings such as digits, margin is
the strongest default routing signal and combined routing can slightly improve the accuracy–cost

silent. The results suggest that uncertainty signals dominate in balanced regimes, while stability
cues become most valuable when escalation must remain extremely rare.
1 Introduction
This paper explores a simple selective-inference idea: instead of relying on a single model for every
input, the system keeps a lightweight always-on controller and activates additional experts only
when the task appears harder. In this view, easy inputs are handled cheaply by the guard model,
while harder inputs trigger a short-lived burst of stronger computation. After the burst, the system
returns to a low-cost mode, similar to a temporary burst of extra computation followed by a return
to the low-cost path.
The central intuition is that not every example deserves the same amount of computation. A guard
can monitor confidence, trigger extra experts when uncertainty rises, aggregate their predictions,
and then deactivate them once the difficult region has passed. This creates a dynamic pipeline with
four ingredients: (i) an always-on guard, (ii) an expert stage that is invoked only when needed,
(iii) a routing signal that estimates hardness, and (iv) an explicit cost trade-off that prevents
constant escalation. Rather than redesigning models themselves, the paper studies a lightweight
orchestration layer that can wrap existing small classical models.
The main question is therefore not whether stronger experts can improve accuracy in principle, but
under what conditions a tiny routing policy can improve the accuracy–efficiency trade-off relative
to strong always-on baselines.
Contributions.This work makes three concrete contributions:
•It develops a lightweight, CPU-only selective routing framework that dynamically switches
between a guard model and stronger experts on a per-sample basis.
•It evaluates margin, wobble, combined, confidence, random, always-expert, and oracle base-
lines under repeated-seed and budget-constrained protocols.
•It shows that routing is regime-dependent: margin is the strongest default signal in balanced
settings, while wobble is the safest ultra-low-budget trigger under extreme class imbalance.

3 Method Overview
The proposed system uses a small guard model to score every input first. If the routing score stays
below a threshold, the guard prediction is returned directly. If the score exceeds that threshold,
the system activates a stronger expert stage and then falls back to low-cost inference on the next
sample. In this sense, the method is best understood as a tiny control policy for selective expert
activation rather than as a new predictive architecture.

3.1 Cost-Aware Objective
The system is trained and selected with a cost-sensitive validation objective. IfT(x)∈ {0,1}is the

router simply calls the expert on almost every sample.
3.2 Routing Signals
We evaluate three routing signals that estimate whether a sample should be escalated to the expert
stage.
Margin.Margin is defined as the difference between the guard’s top two predicted class proba-
bilities. Low margin indicates ambiguity and is treated as a signal of difficulty.
W obble.Wobble measures prediction instability under small input perturbations. For each sam-
ple, repeated noisy perturbations are applied and the fraction of prediction flips is recorded. Higher
wobble indicates greater fragility.

4.2 Digits Stage: When Routing Becomes Competitive
The digits dataset provided the first setting where routing became genuinely competitive. After
strengthening the burst stage, the expert pathway became competent, disagreement became rare,
and the guard stress test showed that the guard was the main bottleneck. This made digits the
cleanest place to study routing selectivity. The detailed diagnostics are in Appendix A.4.
4.3 Signal Study: Margin, Wobble, and Combined Routing
The signal-ablation study isolated the routing rule itself. Margin remained the strongest general-
purpose trigger, wobble captured perturbation fragility, and the combined score slightly reshaped
the frontier in the balanced operating region. The full Pareto tables are in Appendix A.5.
4.4 Cross-Dataset Check
A simple generalization test on digits, wine, and breast cancer showed that routing behavior depends
on guard headroom. Digits offered the clearest benefit, while wine and breast cancer were already
close to solved by the guard. The detailed budget curve is in Appendix A.6.
4.5 Final Rare-Event Benchmark
The final benchmark used the real credit card fraud dataset to test the controller under extreme
class imbalance. The important result was not a dramatic gain over the guard, but that wobble
was the safest ultra-low-budget trigger: when escalation had to remain rare, wobble preserved
guard-level performance while avoiding unnecessary expert calls. Full results are in Appendix A.7.
5 Discussion and Lessons Learned
The main conceptual lesson is simple: the architecture works as a division of labor. A small guard
handles routine cases, and stronger experts are used only when the routing signal suggests that an
input is genuinely difficult. That makes the system a routing layer rather than a new predictive
model, which is the right way to interpret the contribution.
The second lesson is that different signals matter in different regimes. In balanced tasks, margin
remains the strongest general-purpose trigger because it captures uncertainty near the decision
boundary. Wobble is weaker as a standalone signal, but it captures a different property — fragility
under perturbation — and therefore becomes useful as a modifier that can improve the balanced
part of the frontier. This is why the combined signal is interesting: it does not replace margin, but
it can reshape the trade-off curve.
The third lesson is that routing is regime-dependent. When the guard already leaves substantial
headroom, selective escalation can recover most of the expert benefit at lower cost. When the
guard is already strong and the budget is extremely tight, as in the fraud benchmark, the best
policy is often to stay almost silent. That boundary case is valuable because it shows the method
is not universally beneficial; it is beneficial only when the guard, expert, and budget regime are
well matched.

These results suggest a clear next step: instead of adding more signals for their own sake, future

6 Limitations and Future Work
This work has several limitations. First, the experiments are conducted on relatively small datasets
and classical machine-learning models rather than on real embedded deployments with direct power
measurements. Second, routing gains depend strongly on the gap between guard and expert perfor-
mance; when experts are only marginally better, selective escalation offers limited benefit. Third,
even the best routing signals can still over-trigger under some settings, indicating that calibration
and explicit trigger-budget constraints remain open problems.
Future work should therefore focus on stronger hardness predictors, stricter cost constraints, better
guard calibration, and evaluation on genuine low-resource hardware.
7 Conclusion
The experiments in this paper support a simple but useful conclusion: a tiny CPU-only routing
controller can improve the accuracy–efficiency trade-off, but only when the guard, expert, and
trigger are well matched to the task. Raw margin is the best default signal, wobble is a useful
stability cue, and the combined signal is the most interesting frontier-improving variant. The
architecture is therefore viable as a lightweight orchestration layer, not as a new model family. Its
real value is in showing when to spend computation, when to save it, and how to make that choice
in a way that is interpretable, repeatable, and cheap enough to matter.
