Abstract
We study a family of neural activations built around a simple idea: leak need not be treated
only as an error to suppress, but can be used as a bounded auxiliary signal path. The main
formulation is a mixed dual-path activation in which a clean ReLU branch is combined with a
bounded pressure-aware leak branch through learnable path weights. Across the current real-
dataset benchmarks, the mixed formulation is competitive in several settings and consistently
maintains very low dead-unit rates, but it does not uniformly outperform standard baselines. A
focused leak-band sweep further suggests that performance remains stable across a broad range
of target leak values rather than depending on one exact optimum. We therefore present the
paper as an empirical study of controlled auxiliary leak paths: the central claim is not universal
superiority, but that leak can be formalized, learned, and stress-tested as a small auxiliary
computation channel.
1 Introduction
The loose-screw project starts from a simple premise: leak in an activation function need not be
treated only as a flaw. If the leak is bounded, controlled, and learnable, it can be studied as a small
auxiliary signal path rather than as a defect to remove.
The main question in this paper is therefore narrow: can a model use a small leak channel as a
stable helper without losing the benefits of a clean primary path? The main formulation studied
here, Mixed Dual-Path Rattle, is built around that question. Earlier variants such as Pressure
Rattle and Dual-Path Rattle are retained because they explain how the framework developed, but
they are not the main result.
The framework evolves through three steps:
1. a pressure-aware negative-branch prototype,
2. a dual-path formulation with separate clean and leak channels, and
3. a mixed formulation in which the model learns how much each path should contribute.
The contribution is not any single primitive in isolation. ReLU, sigmoid gating, hyperbolic tangent
saturation, and learned weights are all familiar components. The contribution is the way they are
arranged into a leak-aware activation family whose behavior can be measured across real datasets,
depth settings, noise levels, and leak-band sweeps.
More specifically, the paper makes four contributions:
•it defines a leak-aware activation family that progresses from a pressure-aware prototype to
a mixed dual-path formulation with learnable clean and leak contributions;

2 Related Work
The proposed family is related to several established ideas. ReLU introduced sparse activations
and simple gradient flow, while Leaky ReLU and PReLU address dead units by keeping a nonzero
negative slope. Gated activations and mixture-based nonlinearities show that learned routing can
improve representational flexibility. Residual pathways and auxiliary branches similarly support the
idea that a network can benefit from multiple signal routes rather than a single rigid transformation.
The present framework is different in emphasis rather than in any single primitive. It does not
claim novelty for sparsity, gating, bounded saturation, or learned mixing in isolation. Instead, it
treats leak as a bounded auxiliary channel with a learnable contribution, and studies how that
channel behaves under unconstrained training and under forced leak-band sweeps. The empirical
question is therefore not whether leak exists, but how a controlled leak path behaves as a design
choice.
3 Main Method: Mixed Dual-Path Rattle
The main formulation used throughout the paper is theMixed Dual-Path Rattle Activation.
For an inputx, define a clean path and a bounded leak path as follows:
clean(x) = ReLU(x), p=|x|, α(p) =σ(ap+b), λ=σ(ℓ),
leak(x) =λ α(p) tanh(x).
Let the learnable path weights be obtained from logits (θ c, θl) through
[wc, wl] = softmax([θc, θl]).
The final activation is then
f(x) =w c clean(x) +w l leak(x).
This formulation preserves a standard primary signal path while giving the model a second bounded
channel whose contribution can be learned rather than fixed by hand.

3.1 Why the design is stable
The stability of the framework comes from three simple components. The ReLU clean path pre-
serves a strong primary gradient route on positive activations. The sigmoid gate keeps the pressure
response bounded between 0 and 1. The hyperbolic tangent term keeps the auxiliary path smooth,
sign-aware, and bounded, preventing it from exploding at large magnitude. In the mixed for-
mulation, the softmax path weights further constrain the clean and leak contributions to remain
normalized and nonnegative.
3.2 Interpretation
The model does not treat leak as the dominant signal. Instead, leak is used as a small, learned
helper path. That is the interpretation that emerges repeatedly in the experiments: the auxiliary
channel is not deleted, but it usually remains small.
4 Main Results
The main benchmark combines four real datasets: Wisconsin Breast Cancer, Wine, Digits, and
Diabetes. Across three random seeds, the mixed formulation is competitive in several classification
settings, especially where dead units matter, but it is not uniformly best. Its strongest results are
not raw accuracy wins; they are stable training behavior, very low dead-unit rates, and consis-
tent evidence that useful leak values occupy a task-dependent band rather than a single universal
optimum.
The most important pattern is therefore not that Mixed Dual-Path Rattle beats every baseline
everywhere. It is that the model usually preserves activity, remains trainable, and learns a small
auxiliary contribution rather than collapsing the leak path to zero. On classification tasks, the
mixed model is often close to the strongest baseline. On regression and deeper settings, the advan-
tage is weaker and sometimes absent.
4.1 Dead units and stability
One of the clearest results is the dead-unit comparison. ReLU shows noticeable dead-unit rates
that increase with depth, especially on Breast Cancer, Wine, Digits, and Diabetes. Leaky ReLU
maintains essentially zero dead units throughout. Mixed Dual-Path Rattle behaves much closer to
Leaky ReLU than to ReLU: on most runs its dead-unit rate is exactly zero, and even when nonzero
it remains small relative to ReLU.
The important point is that the mixed formulation appears to preserve the activity-protection
benefit of having a nonzero auxiliary path, even when that path receives only a small learned
weight.

4.2 Robustness to input noise
Under additive test-time input noise, Mixed Dual-Path Rattle is sometimes competitive but not
uniformly more robust. On Breast Cancer, mean noisy accuracy is slightly better than Leaky ReLU
at moderate noise and remains close to the baselines at larger noise. On Wine and Digits, the mixed
model often trails Leaky ReLU once noise is added. On Diabetes, all methods change only slightly
under the tested noise levels, and Mixed Dual-Path Rattle remains worse than Leaky ReLU.
So the present evidence supports a narrower claim: the mixed formulation can remain stable under
noise, but the current implementation does not establish a general noise-robustness advantage over
simpler baselines.
4.3 Learned path mixtures
The learned mixture weights are especially informative. Across all four datasets and all tested
depths, the mixed model consistently assigns the vast majority of weight to the clean path and
only a small but nonzero weight to the leak path. The clean-path weight is typically around 0.95–
0.96 on the classification datasets and about the same on Diabetes, leaving only a few percent for
the leak path.
This is important because it gives a concrete interpretation of the framework: in the unconstrained
mixed model, the leak path is used consistently, but sparingly. Validation loss decreases steadily
while the learned mixture remains nearly flat, with the clean weight staying near 0.95 and the leak
weight near 0.04–0.05 throughout training. Meanwhile,λstays close to 0.10 and the average gate
valueαrises gradually over training, indicating that the model mainly adjusts the gate statistics
while preserving a strongly clean-dominant mixture.
At the same time, the broader forced-leak experiments show that this natural small-leak preference
should not be interpreted as a universal optimum. When the leak level is fixed externally and
swept over a wide range, performance often remains competitive well beyond the natural 4–5%
setting. The more defensible interpretation is therefore two-part: unconstrained optimization tends
to settle on a small auxiliary leak, but the family as a whole appears to operate within a broader
task-dependent leak band rather than around a single magic value.
4.4 Task summary
The overall pattern is cautious rather than dramatic. Mixed Dual-Path Rattle is viable and often
competitive, but it is not uniformly best. On Breast Cancer it essentially ties the strongest baselines
while almost eliminating dead units. On Digits it is competitive at shallow depth but loses ground
at depth 6. On Wine it matches the baselines at depth 2 but degrades more strongly with additional
depth. On Diabetes regression it underperforms Leaky ReLU across all depths, although it still
preserves very low dead-unit rates.
These numbers suggest a modest but fairly consistent picture. The mixed formulation looks most
promising on shallow-to-medium-depth classification settings, but it is not yet a reliable drop-in

different: moderate or small target leaks performed best, and the strongest forced configurations
were competitive with the standard baselines.
The noise sweeps reinforce the same idea. Higher evaluation noise did not fundamentally reorder
the preferred leak band within each task. Instead, configurations that were already strong at
zero noise usually remained strong under noise, which suggests that the target leak is acting more
like a structural bias on representation than like a separate robustness knob that can be tuned
independently through penalty strength.
The main lesson from these benchmarks is that the loose-screw family does not appear to have a
single universal best leak value. Rather, different tasks prefer different leak bands: very small leak
for some tabular classification settings, moderate leak for some Breast Cancer settings, and larger
leak values can remain viable on Digits without causing collapse. This strengthens the framework
interpretation considerably. The useful question is no longer simply whether leak should be near
zero, but which controlled leak band is most appropriate for a given regime.
5 Interpretation and Scope
The central empirical finding of this study is that the leak-aware family exhibits a broad, task-
dependent operating region rather than a single privileged leak value. In unconstrained mixed
models, optimization naturally settles on a small auxiliary leak of roughly 4–5%. But when the
leak is fixed externally and swept across a much wider range, performance often remains strong
over a broad interval instead of collapsing away from that natural equilibrium. This makes the
main significance of the work more specific than a broad performance claim. Its value is that it
identifies and formalizes a design pattern in which leak is treated as a bounded auxiliary signal
path rather than only as an error term.
The strongest current claim should therefore be limited and defensible. The mathematical ingre-

dients are familiar: ReLU, sigmoid gating, hyperbolic tangent saturation, additive side paths, and
softmax mixing. What is new here is their arrangement into a leak-aware activation family in which
pressure-aware leak is explicitly modeled, separated, and then mixed with the primary path in a
learnable way.
A defensible description is the following: this is anovel activation-family directionin which leak
is elevated from a passive residual effect to a controlled auxiliary channel, and in which the mixed
and additive formulations let the network regulate how much of that auxiliary channel should be
used. Empirically, the clearest current result is not universal superiority, but the combination of two
behaviors: a natural tendency toward small learned leak under unconstrained mixing, and a broader
leak band within which forced leak remains viable. The supplementary deep-CNN and MNIST
follow-ups fit that interpretation as diagnostic evidence rather than as headline wins: they mainly
indicate that once optimization is stabilized, especially by normalization, the mixed formulation
remains viable in harder regimes without clearly surpassing the strongest baseline.
This avoids overclaiming. It does not say that every component is individually unprecedented, nor
does it claim universal superiority over standard activations. Instead, it claims that the family
perspective, the progression of variants, and the observed leak-band behavior together constitute a
real and testable contribution.
6 Limitations
The current evidence is useful but not complete. The benchmarks are still limited in scope, and the
strongest results are not consistently better than strong baselines. The mixed formulation is best
viewed as an interpretable and empirically testable family rather than as a universal replacement
for standard activations.
Three limitations matter most. First, the evaluation is broad enough to reveal patterns, but
still too small to support claims about modern large-scale training regimes. Second, the paper
compares against strong baseline activations but not against a wider set of modern gated or adaptive
alternatives. Third, several of the most interesting observations—especially the broader leak-band
behavior and the deep-network diagnostics—are empirical regularities rather than consequences of
a formal theory.
The current results also suggest that a better empirical question is not simply whether leak helps,
but when and how much leak helps. That is why the leak-band view is central: the family appears
to admit a useful operating region rather than a single best value.
7 Next Experimental Direction
The next research question is no longer whether the idea can be written down mathematically. It
is whether the leak-aware family has a useful operating region and whether that region remains
stable in harder settings.

The most useful next steps are:
•sweep the target leak level directly, from very small to moderately large values, to test whether
the framework has a useful leak band rather than a single universal optimum;
•compare fixed, learnable, and per-neuron leak settings;
•test deeper networks and harder datasets, especially noisy-label and small-data regimes;
•track the learned values ofa,b,λ, and the path weights throughout training; and
•extend the framework beyond activations into leak-aware blocks or lightweight residual-style

This progression keeps the work grounded: formalize the variants that were actually run, report
what the benchmark really shows, and use the current results as a base for more targeted experi-
ments.
8 Conclusion
The current paper does not establish a universally superior activation. Instead, it establishes a
more limited but still meaningful result: leak can be treated as a bounded auxiliary signal path,
and the resulting family behaves in a controlled and task-dependent way.
The strongest current evidence is that the mixed dual-path formulation naturally learns a small
leak contribution, while forced leak-band sweeps show that performance can remain stable across a
broad range of leak values. The supplementary deep-network diagnostics point in the same direction
but should be read more cautiously: they mainly show that the formulation remains analyzable in
harder regimes, and that optimization choices such as normalization can dominate outcomes there.
Taken together, these results support treating the loose-screw family not as a single magical acti-
vation, but as a structured framework for studying how small auxiliary signal channels behave in
neural networks. The main takeaway is therefore conceptual as much as empirical: a leak channel
can be present, bounded, and useful without becoming the dominant path, and that controlled
auxiliary role is measurable across both unconstrained training and forced leak sweeps.

Across three seeds, the mixed formulation achieved mean test accuracy about 0.4016±0.0099,
compared with about 0.3858±0.0076 for Leaky ReLU and about 0.3840±0.0229 for ReLU. The
dead-unit pattern was sharper: ReLU reached mean dead-unit rate about 0.1376, whereas both
Leaky ReLU and the mixed formulation remained at 0.0000 under the same threshold. In the
mixed model, the learned path weights again stayed strongly clean-dominant, with mean clean-
path weight about 0.9455 and mean leak-path weight about 0.0545.
This result should still be interpreted cautiously. It does not show universal superiority, but it does
strengthen one narrower part of the framework claim: in this very deep non-residual setting, the
mixed auxiliary-path formulation remains trainable, preserves activity across all layers, and stays
competitive with the standard baselines.

D.3 Depth-25CNN follow-up: initialization versus normalization
To probe the breaking-point result more directly, we ran a focused follow-up at depth 25 on the
same plain non-residual CNN, now comparing eight variants: ReLU, Leaky ReLU, and the mixed
activation with either a roughly 95/5 clean–leak initialization or a 50/50 initialization, each with
and without batch normalization. The question was narrower than in the earlier appendix exper-
iment: is the poor depth-25 behavior mainly a consequence of the mixed-path initialization, or is
normalization the larger factor?
The answer from this run is suggestive rather than definitive. Without batch normalization, all

four plain variants remained weak and unstable. Mean test accuracy was about 0.1801±0.0717
for ReLU, 0.2164±0.1065 for Leaky ReLU, 0.1416±0.0721 for the mixed 95/5 model, and exactly
chance-level 0.1000 for the mixed 50/50 model. Dead-unit rates were also substantial in the plain
setting, especially for the 50/50 mixed initialization, which reached mean dead rate about 0.4000.
So changing the initial clean–leak balance alone does not rescue the deep plain CNN; in fact, the
symmetric initialization makes the no-batch-norm case worse.
With batch normalization, however, the picture changes materially. All four normalized variants
trained reliably, all reached zero measured dead units under the same threshold, and their accuracies
clustered in a much narrower band: about 0.4617±0.0121 for ReLU, 0.4374±0.0213 for Leaky
ReLU, 0.4309±0.0185 for mixed 95/5, and 0.4364±0.0258 for mixed 50/50. This does not make the
mixed formulation the best performer in the normalized regime, but it does suggest that the depth-

idea itself.
The learned mixtures add a useful qualification. In the no-batch-norm runs, the mixed 95/5 model
stayed essentially fixed at its initial allocation, while the 50/50 model remained near parity and
drifted slightly toward the leak path in later layers, yet still failed completely. In the batch-
normalized runs, the mixed 95/5 model again stayed strongly clean-dominant, whereas the mixed
50/50 model learned a broader layerwise redistribution while still training successfully. That com-

main stabilizer here, while the mixed activation remains viable once the surrounding optimization
problem is made tractable.
The noise sweep is consistent with that reading. Over test-time Gaussian noise levels from 0.0 to

remained stronger than the plain ones throughout, and there was no evidence that the mixed
variants gained a unique robustness advantage in this particular follow-up.

D.4 MNIST label-corruption stress test
As a final supplementary check, we ran a CPU-friendly label-corruption stress test on MNIST
using a three-hidden-layer multilayer perceptron with widths 512, 256, and 128. We compared
ReLU, Leaky ReLU, Mixed Dual-Path Rattle with a roughly 95/5 initialization, and Mixed Dual-
Path Rattle with a 50/50 initialization over three seeds and five epochs. The training labels were
corrupted at rates 0.0, 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7, while evaluation used the clean validation and test labels.
This experiment should be read as a targeted corruption stress test rather than as a new headline
benchmark.
The main pattern is straightforward. On clean MNIST, all four activations were essentially tied
near 0.96 test accuracy. As label noise increased, the mixed 50/50 model degraded more slowly
than the others. At noise 0.3, it reached mean test accuracy slightly above the other three variants.
At noise 0.5, the gap widened: mixed 50/50 was ahead of Leaky ReLU, ReLU, and mixed 95/5.
At noise 0.7, mixed 50/50 remained best, with the other methods trailing behind.
Two additional diagnostics are noteworthy. First, the dead-rate pattern remained benign for all
non-ReLU variants: Leaky ReLU and both mixed models stayed at zero under the chosen threshold

===== PAGE 27 =====
across all tested noise levels, while ReLU retained a small but nonzero dead rate. Second, the
mixed 50/50 model trained with substantially smaller gradient norms than the other activations
throughout the sweep. That behavior is consistent with a more damped optimization profile under
severe label corruption, although this experiment alone does not establish the mechanism.
The mixed-path diagnostics make the comparison more specific. The mixed 95/5 model stayed
strongly clean-dominant across all layers and all noise levels, with clean weight near 0.95 and leak
weight near 0.05, and it did not show a corruption advantage over the standard baselines. By
contrast, the mixed 50/50 model maintained a substantially larger auxiliary contribution, with
clean and leak weights staying close to parity across layers. In this particular MNIST corruption
setting, that more balanced allocation was associated with the strongest performance at the higher
noise levels.
This supplementary result is useful because it identifies a narrower regime in which the framework
may matter more: severely corrupted supervision in a simple dense network. At the same time, it
should still be interpreted conservatively. It does not show a universal robustness advantage, and
it does not imply that larger leak is generally better. What it does show is that under this specific
label-corruption stress test, the mixed formulation with a much less clean-dominant initialization
remained competitive on clean data and became the strongest of the tested activations as the label
noise grew extreme.
D.5 MNIST extreme label-corruption extension
We also extended the same MNIST setup to even harsher label-corruption rates of 0.8 and 0.9,
using ten epochs on CPU. This extension is useful because it helps distinguish two regimes: one
in which the task is still difficult but learnable, and another in which the supervision becomes so
corrupted that all methods are pushed close to chance behavior.
At noise 0.8, the mixed family remained competitive. Mean test accuracy was high for all methods,
with the mixed variants remaining in the same general range as ReLU and Leaky ReLU. At noise
0.9, the problem is close to broken for every activation. Mean test accuracy falls near chance for
all four methods. In other words, this regime does not provide an honest accuracy-win story for
the mixed formulation. Its main value is diagnostic.
That diagnostic signal is still informative. ReLU accumulates large dead-unit rates as the corrup-
tion becomes extreme, while Leaky ReLU and both mixed variants stay at zero under the chosen
threshold. The mixed models also retain smaller mean gradient norms than the standard baselines,
especially for the 50/50 initialization. Those patterns are consistent with the leak-aware family
acting as a more stability-oriented activation under severe corruption, even when that stability
does not always translate into higher accuracy.
The layerwise mixtures sharpen that interpretation. Mixed 95/5 stays strongly clean-dominant at
both extreme noise levels, with weights remaining near 0.95/0.05 across all three layers. Mixed
50/50 behaves differently: at noise 0.8 it stays moderately clean-dominant, whereas at noise 0.9 it
shifts to a leak-heavier allocation. That shift is interesting as a learned response to a near-broken
training signal, but the poor accuracy at 0.9 means it should be treated as a diagnostic observation
rather than as evidence of successful adaptation.
